Using AI Without Giving Up the Thinking

A candid reflection on AI as a tutor, debugger, and brainstorming partner—and why the responsibility for understanding still remains mine.

December 16, 20248 minute readReflection
ai-and-my-learning.logPERSONAL_NOTE

AI became part of how I learned software engineering, but its value depended on how I used it. It could explain a concept in a different way, help me interpret an error, or give me a small example to experiment with. It could also be confidently wrong, miss the context of an assignment, or offer code I did not truly understand.

A tutor I can question repeatedly

One advantage of an AI tutor is the ability to ask follow-up questions without worrying that I am taking too much time. When React’s useEffect felt confusing, I could request a step-by-step explanation, then ask why a specific dependency changed the behavior. That conversation gave me another path into documentation that initially felt dense.

The useful part was not copying the example. It was changing it, testing it, and comparing the result with my expectation. The loop between explanation and experimentation is where the learning happened.

Debugging still requires judgment

Error messages can be overwhelming when I do not yet know which part matters. AI helped me translate some of those messages into possible causes. But a possible cause is not a diagnosis. I still had to inspect my code, test one hypothesis at a time, and confirm what actually fixed the issue.

That distinction became important during team projects. Generated boilerplate could save time, but missing error handling or inefficient asynchronous logic could create new work. Treating a suggestion as finished code would have hidden those problems.

AI works best for me as a guide and a starting point—not as permission to stop thinking.

When I choose not to use it

Timed exercises and practice problems are sometimes more valuable without assistance. Struggling through them exposes what I can actually recall and where my understanding is weak. If I reach for an answer too quickly, I lose that feedback.

Balance matters. There are moments when speed and explanation are useful, and moments when independent effort is the whole purpose of the task.

My responsibility stays the same

Whether I use documentation, a classmate, a search engine, or AI, I am responsible for the work I submit and the claims I make. I need to verify outputs, protect private information, respect course rules, and be able to explain the result in my own words.

AI has made some concepts more approachable and helped me move past unproductive confusion. The goal, however, is not simply to finish faster. It is to become the kind of learner who knows how to ask better questions and how to recognize when an answer needs more work.
