How AI Became My Guide, Not My Shortcut

What stressful WODs, stubborn bugs, and a lot of follow-up questions taught me about learning with AI while keeping the thinking my own.

Justine AfagaComputer Science graduate student · Hawaiʻi
Published Dec 16, 2024Revised Aug 14, 20269 minute read
ai-and-my-learning.logPERSONAL_NOTE

When I first started using ChatGPT in ICS 314, I felt a little uncomfortable. Software engineering was already challenging, and I did not want a tool generating everything for me before I understood it myself. At the same time, the WODs were stressful, the error messages were not always clear, and sometimes I needed an explanation that I could question more than once. The hard part was not deciding whether AI was good or bad. It was learning when it helped me think and when it was starting to think for me.

WHAT YOU’LL TAKE AWAY

AI became most useful when I treated its answers as material to question, test, and rewrite—not as finished work that I could trust without understanding.

E18 changed the way I used AI

One of my hardest WODs was E18, which focused on functional programming and underscore.js. I struggled with methods like .pluck and .map, especially when I had to chain several operations together under a time limit.

I asked ChatGPT for a basic example of using .pluck with an array of student objects. That example gave me enough confidence to begin, but it did not solve the whole problem. Once I had to combine filtering, mapping, and chaining, some suggestions only partly matched the assignment. I still had to change the code, run it, see what broke, and compare the result with what I expected.

Looking back, that was the important part. The first answer saved me from staring at a blank screen, but the experimenting afterward is what helped the concept stay with me.

AI works best for me when it helps me enter the problem—not when it removes the problem before I have learned from it.

The best part was being able to ask again

When I was learning React, useEffect felt confusing. I could read a tutorial and still have questions about why the dependency array changed the behavior or why a component ran more than once. With AI, I could ask for a simpler explanation and then immediately ask a more specific follow-up question.

For me, that felt like having a patient tutor available while I worked. Still, the explanation only became meaningful when I opened my code, changed one thing, and watched what happened. The conversation helped make the documentation less intimidating; testing helped me know whether I actually understood it.

Debugging taught me not to trust the first answer

AI was especially useful when an error message felt overwhelming. It could translate the message into a few possible causes and give me a place to start. But a possible cause is not the same as a diagnosis.

During class projects, suggestions sometimes missed error handling, ignored an edge case, or used logic that did not fit our application. I learned to test one possibility at a time and confirm what actually fixed the problem. If I changed three things because a chatbot listed three ideas, I might get working code without learning which change mattered.

Sometimes the best choice is to close the AI tab

Timed WODs made this very clear. The goal was not only to finish; it was to see what I could recall and apply under pressure. Reaching for AI too quickly could distract me or hide the exact gap I needed to practice.

I did not always get that balance right. Sometimes I used help when I wanted to work independently, and sometimes I struggled longer than I needed to. But those moments taught me to pause and ask what the assignment is really trying to develop. If the purpose is practice, some frustration is part of the learning.

The rules I use now

MY AI CHECKLIST
  1. Explain the problem before asking for an answer.If I cannot describe what I tried and where I am stuck, I probably need to inspect the problem more carefully first.
  2. Ask for a hint or a small example.A starting point gives me room to continue thinking instead of handing me a complete solution.
  3. Test every suggestion.I run the code, check edge cases, and compare the behavior with the assignment requirements.
  4. Rewrite and explain it in my own words.If I cannot explain why the answer works, I am not ready to use it.
  5. Respect the boundaries.I follow course rules and avoid sharing private information, credentials, or material that should not leave the project.

AI also changed how I think about writing

I have used AI to brainstorm essay ideas, organize questions, and look at a topic from another direction. The risk is that a polished answer can sound finished before it sounds like me. My own writing is usually more direct. I talk about what frustrated me, what I tried, and what changed after I struggled with it.

Revising this post reminded me that writing is also a form of understanding. I can use a tool to help me see the structure, but I still need to choose the examples, make the connection, and say what I really learned. That is how the final piece becomes mine.

ONE QUESTION TO KEEP

Can you explain the answer without the tool?

Before keeping an AI suggestion, close the chat and explain what the code or idea is doing in your own words. The parts that feel difficult to explain are the parts worth studying next.
