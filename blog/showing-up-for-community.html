JOURNAL / COMMUNITY / 03

Why Showing Up Changed How I Want to Build

Lessons from Leo Club, Tagnawa, and growing up in Hawaiʻi that now shape the kind of technologist—and person—I want to become.

Justine AfagaComputer Science graduate student · Hawaiʻi
Published Aug 11, 2026Revised Aug 14, 20268 minute read
showing-up-for-community.logPERSONAL_NOTE

I used to think community service mostly meant signing up for an event, doing the assigned job, and going home knowing I helped. Growing up on Hawaiʻi Island and working with groups like the Konawaena Leo Club and Tagnawa made that idea more personal. I learned that service is not one impressive moment. Most of the time, it is listening carefully, doing ordinary work, and showing up again when people are counting on you.

WHAT YOU’LL TAKE AWAY

The experiences that shaped me outside the classroom also shape how I approach computer science: listen before solving, understand the people involved, and measure a project by whether it is actually useful to them.

Leo Club taught me that service starts with ordinary work

From 2019 through 2022, the Konawaena Leo Club gave me opportunities to help with tree planting, night markets, and support for kūpuna. The activities were different, but they all depended on the same thing: people arriving, sharing the small tasks, and staying engaged after the exciting part was over.

At first, I thought leadership meant being the person in front. Through the club, I started to see it as something quieter. Leadership could mean setting up, checking whether another person needed help, or doing a job that nobody would notice unless it was left unfinished.

Those moments taught me reliability. A community event works because many people decide that their small responsibility still matters.

Showing up is not only being present. It is paying enough attention to notice what the people around you actually need.

Tagnawa taught me to listen before trying to help

In 2023, I supported Tagnawa’s Hawak Kamay outreach and call-center work for Filipino community members affected by the Lahaina fires and the October reopening. The work required patience and careful listening. A person calling for information was not an abstract case. They were living through uncertainty, loss, and decisions that affected their family.

I learned that useful help begins with respecting what another person is asking for. Organization and technology can support that response, but they cannot replace human care. Sometimes the most important thing I could do was slow down, listen, and make sure the information I shared was clear.

My culture and my computer science work are connected

Studying Computer Science and Philippine Language and Culture gave me two ways to think about the same goal. I want to understand systems, but I also want to understand the people inside those systems—their language, history, needs, and community.

As a Filipino-American born and raised in Hawaiʻi, I do not want to separate technology from the place and people who shaped me. I am interested in AI and software because they can help solve real problems. But a technically impressive solution is not enough if it ignores the people it is supposed to serve.

For me, people-centered technology means asking more questions before I decide what to build. Who is this for? What do they already use? What might make the solution difficult to trust or access? The answers may change the design more than any new feature.

What I want to carry into every project

A PEOPLE-FIRST CHECKLIST
  • Listen before deciding on the solution.The first version of a problem is not always the whole story.
  • Learn the local context.Language, place, history, access, and culture affect whether a tool will feel useful.
  • Explain things clearly.A system only helps if people can understand what it does and what they are being asked to do.
  • Build with people, not only for them.Feedback should shape the work while it is being made, not arrive after every decision is finished.
  • Measure usefulness, not only completion.Finishing a feature matters, but the stronger question is whether it made something better for the person using it.

A second life, not a separate life

My family, culture, friendships, and community work are not distractions from becoming a computer scientist. They are the reason I care what technology does. They remind me that growth is not only the number of languages I know or projects I can list. It is also whether I become more thoughtful, dependable, and useful to other people.

I am still learning what that looks like in the software I will build. I know I will not always get it right. But I want to keep asking who benefits, who may be left out, and whether I listened long enough before I started coding.

Looking back, the lesson sounds simple: show up, listen, and do the work in front of you. Living that lesson consistently is the part that keeps shaping me.

A QUESTION FOR YOUR NEXT PROJECT

Who did you listen to before you started building?

If you are working on something for a community, ask one person from that community what would make it genuinely useful. Their answer may change the whole direction in a good way.
